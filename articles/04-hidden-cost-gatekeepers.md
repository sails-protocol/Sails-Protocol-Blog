## The shift

Regulatory compliance is not inherently an enemy of innovation. Safeguards against fraud, misuse and insolvency matter. Yet fixed costs of licensing, legal review, risk management and reporting may be easier to absorb at scale. This can make the market more concentrated without proving that regulation is the sole cause. Case-by-case evidence is necessary before attributing an exchange closure or a foreign exit to a particular rule.

## The design response

A modular marketplace can let smaller teams specialize in interfaces, discovery or analytics without replicating every function of an exchange. Specialization, however, is not exemption: operators performing regulated brokerage, payments or custody must assess their own obligations. Sails seeks to reduce unnecessary coupling, not make regulated activities invisible.

## A failure worth testing

If only large firms can afford duplicated compliance and custody infrastructure, smaller competitors may be excluded even when their software solves a legitimate customer problem.

## The price of fixed obligations

Capital and compliance requirements can protect customers while also changing which business models remain economically viable. A large group may spread legal, audit and reporting costs across many services. A specialized brokerage may not have the same margin. Coinext and Bity make that trade-off tangible through their own closure notices, but two examples do not measure the entire Brazilian market.

The missing evidence is as important as the visible exits: market shares before and after the authorization process, the number of authorized providers, and the fate of customers who moved to surviving platforms. Until those data exist, concentration should be framed as a risk or plausible mechanism, not a proven numerical outcome.

## Costs do not disappear in an open network

An open architecture may distribute infrastructure expenses and lower some barriers, but it also creates costs for dispute handling, security, uptime and compliance. The useful comparison is not free versus expensive. It is which costs are unavoidable, who bears them and whether participants can switch providers without abandoning their trading history.

## Who pays for the gate

A marketplace can impose entry costs in several ways: legal onboarding, minimum balance requirements, proprietary market data, restricted API access and mandatory infrastructure vendors. Not all of these costs are illegitimate. Some finance fraud prevention, customer support and operational resilience. The question is whether a participant can evaluate them in advance, whether smaller operators face disproportionate burdens and whether changing providers requires surrendering identity, reputation or transaction history. A shared coordination protocol may reduce the cost of connecting independent front ends, but it cannot erase the legal obligations of a company offering regulated services.

A fair comparison would measure more than transaction fees. It would include the time needed to integrate a second wallet, the cost of resolving a disputed trade, the expense of recovering from an outage and the degree of dependence on one administrator. If the supposedly open network merely replaces one centralized dependency with an indispensable indexer, dispute operator or liquidity venue, the gatekeeper has changed names rather than disappeared. For Sails, this is a design hypothesis to test with multiple independent implementations, not a measured market outcome.

## What a fair comparison would actually measure

Imagine two teams offering the same kind of peer-to-peer transaction. One runs a vertically integrated platform; the other uses a shared coordination layer and independently chosen providers. A meaningful comparison would start with the complete cost of serving a customer, not merely the fee displayed at checkout. Both teams must pay for support, security, dispute handling and legal analysis appropriate to their activities. The second team might save on duplicated discovery infrastructure, but it might pay more to coordinate several providers and to manage failures between them.

The decisive experiment is portability. Can a user move to another interface without being forced to surrender control of funds or restart a reputation record? Can an operator replace an unavailable service without rewriting its entire business? And can an auditor trace which party actually accepted each obligation? Those questions are measurable; none is answered merely by calling a product decentralized.

## Evidence before conclusions

Brazilian company announcements are useful primary records of what each firm said it would do, not proof of the reasons every firm made its decision. The Coinext and Bity notices should be read alongside the Central Bank's applicable regulatory text. A stronger market-wide claim would also require a dated series of provider counts, transaction volumes and market shares, using consistent definitions before and after the regulatory transition. Until such a series is assembled, the argument here remains about incentives and design trade-offs rather than an observed increase in concentration.
