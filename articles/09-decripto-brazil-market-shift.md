## A market can grow while some of its builders leave

For years, the debate about regulating crypto in Brazil focused on a hypothetical choice between innovation and consumer protection. In 2026 the trade-offs became concrete. Established operators announced that their economics no longer justified continuing their retail brokerage business under the new authorization framework. That is not the same as saying they failed their customers or went bankrupt. It is a question about the structure of the market that survives.

<!-- truncate -->

## Coinext: capital as a hard boundary

Coinext, founded in 2017, announced an orderly withdrawal from crypto brokerage. Its own FAQ says the Brazilian framework required minimum regulatory capital for an authorization request; the company did not reach that minimum and therefore saw no economic basis for filing. Its plan provides for returning customers' assets and keeping BRL withdrawals available. Its public communication says it was not insolvent. This is direct, unusually clear evidence of regulatory entry conditions shaping a real business decision.

## Bity: an established platform chooses a different future

Bity, formerly associated with the Bitybank brand, announced that it would stop operating its retail crypto brokerage rather than invest in the dedicated capital and infrastructure required under the new rules. Its own notice recounts years of operations and a network of exchange partners. It provides an orderly transition and options to migrate to Mercado Bitcoin or Bipa. The company says it is a strategic decision, not a collapse. The consequence for market structure remains worth asking: where do customers and trading relationships go when a familiar intermediary closes?

## Foreign platforms face a different choice

The BCB's Resolution 520 addresses foreign entities operating in Brazil and imposes a transition process for those seeking to continue. Bybit announced in May 2026 that eligible Brazilian users would be migrated to a locally based entity. This is localization, not evidence that Bybit abandoned Brazil. Bitso’s own notice confirms a retail-service transition toward Mercado Bitcoin, not a complete exit from Brazil. Other exits require separate evidence.

## Bitso: leaving direct retail service, not disappearing from Brazil

A third case brings an international operator into focus. In its official help-center notice, Bitso confirms that it will stop serving Brazilian retail customers directly by October 30. Its announced arrangement with Mercado Bitcoin provides a transition route for accounts and balances. This is not evidence of bankruptcy or a complete exit from every Brazilian business line. It is evidence of a concrete change in who provides the direct consumer-facing service.

This distinction matters when discussing market concentration. Counting international brand names is not the same as measuring independent retail competitors. Analysts must establish who holds the customer relationship, custody, liquidity and execution authority. Bitso's announcement documents a commercial restructuring; it does not by itself establish a market-share change or prove that any single regulation was the sole cause.

## Does regulation concentrate the market?

Higher fixed compliance costs can favor larger companies that can finance capital, governance and reporting. The two confirmed brokerage exits and the localization of a foreign platform illustrate that mechanism, but they do not alone establish a numerical increase in concentration. To make that stronger claim we would need before-and-after market shares, volumes, customer migrations and licensing outcomes. Consumer protection and prevention of financial crime remain legitimate reasons for regulation; the question is whether the market can also preserve space for independent software businesses.

## Where Sails enters the picture

Sails Protocol proposes an Economic Coordination Protocol for building interoperable P2P financial marketplaces. The design aims to let wallets and applications discover opportunities, coordinate negotiations and exchange evidence without requiring the protocol software provider to hold customer balances. The first focus is the P2P Trading SDK. The other modules are a roadmap, not a claim of deployed infrastructure.

The distinction matters: developing noncustodial coordination software may have a different legal characterization from operating a custodial brokerage, but the word *noncustodial* is not a blanket exemption. An application can still perform regulated payment, exchange, brokerage or derivatives activities. Actual control over signing, funds, execution, settlement and fees must be assessed for each operator and jurisdiction.

The failure mode is equally practical. If two applications accept the last capacity of one offer, or if a node reports settlement without external evidence, a supposedly open market has recreated the risks of a centralized operator without the corresponding safeguards. Sails' architectural claims must be tested against concurrent execution, idempotency, authority and payment evidence.

## The next question

If established intermediaries can no longer justify every function of a vertically integrated exchange, what infrastructure allows new participants to build lawful, economically viable marketplaces without controlling every user's assets? That is the question Sails is built to investigate, not a promise that the law stops at the edge of an SDK.

## A dated snapshot, not a verdict

The Brazilian market is changing through distinct decisions: closures, local migrations and retail-service transitions. The articles should preserve the dates and language of each primary notice. They should not turn a collection of cases into a market-share statistic, a bankruptcy finding or proof that regulation alone caused every decision.
